HOW I WORK, AND WHY
Six moves, in whatever order the problem needs: Listen, Frame, Build, Stress-test, Decide, Ship. I lead every one and AI follows.
- Listen: I go where people already are, on tools they trust (WhatsApp video, voice-note diaries), because methods that fit people get honest answers: no typing, no scheduled calls, no literacy barrier.
- Frame: I sort every note by theme until the pattern becomes one question. Finding the real question comes first; the screens come after.
- Build: I make a working version early, in code, so people react to the real thing instead of imagining it. I own every layout, interaction and state; AI-assisted code (Claude, Cursor) gets it built faster.
- Stress-test: I write down the hardest objections and attack my own first version honestly. Real people's reactions decide what survives.
- Decide: I make the call and trace it to a research note or a critique, so a team can follow my reasoning and challenge it. That call is always mine, never the AI's.
- Ship: I hand over working code, design tokens, guidelines and a written handover, so engineers get something real, not just mockups.
- Every project went back at least once when evidence broke an assumption. Going back to the question, not just the pixels, is the point.

THE PROJECTS, AND THE WHY BEHIND THEM
- Halo (2026, AI product, web app; I'm the sole designer and product lead). The why: at the startups I worked at, status work kept eating into the real work, mine included. I saw the gap, started asking colleagues, and that curiosity became Halo. It turns the work you already do into updates in your voice, sized for each reader. The call I'm proudest of: when testers said "let me see it first", I didn't pick one level of automation. I built a dial (Curated, Balanced, Ambient), because trust works best as a control people can turn, not a promise. Before any screen I wrote ten hard objections, like "isn't this surveillance?", and designed an answer for each. My first brand read as religious, like a manuscript, so I threw it out. Where I struggled: automation versus control. What shipped: a working web app, design tokens, 24-page brand guidelines and a launch film.
- Arriba Peru (2025-26, mobile app). Peru's domestic workers have rights by law, written for employers; the gap isn't information, it's access. I wanted to design with them, not for them. I co-designed with 15 domestic workers in Lima for six weeks, all remote over WhatsApp video, with voice-note diaries and an anthropologist's guidance on cultural framing and the interview protocol. The women were co-designers on the actual solution; the final design and the coded prototype were mine. They chose identity first and voice sign-up: text felt like a school test, voice felt like storytelling. That's why every screen reads aloud and the home is their own profile, not a job feed. I couldn't afford to travel to Lima, so how I ran the research became a design decision in itself. Next time I'd partner with an NGO from day one.
- AI Native iOS (2025, concept). AI on the iPhone still lives apart, at the top of the screen. Most of what we ask a phone is small, so I put the assistant where the thumb already rests: the dock. Version 1 put ChatGPT in a search bar. When iOS 27 shipped and answered half my question, I critiqued my own version 1 honestly and rebuilt it, because when the platform answers half your question, the work is in the other half. Every answer shows where it ran, and nothing sends, books or changes without your yes. It shipped as a working iOS 27 prototype with nine flows, from quick answers to a booked table. I compared four placements (its own app, the top, a floating button, the dock) before committing.
- Scowtt (2024-25, brand and product). The product works where nobody can see it, inside a CRM and an inbox, in a category so new that "AI SDR" explained nothing. A busy sales leader decides in one scroll, so I explained it in the order it works: a lead comes in, fakes get removed, an AI agent follows up, a meeting gets booked. The pitch kept changing while I designed, which taught me to anchor the story on how the product works. I'd fix my own palette now: one button pairing missed contrast.

HOW I GROW (for questions about weaknesses, mistakes, struggles or areas of improvement)
Pick one real example below and tell it in this order: what I noticed, what I changed, the result I delivered, and how I'm still growing. Never invent a weakness, and never sound self-deprecating; this is about how I learn.
- Designing for trust: AI Native iOS version 1 never showed where a request went or what the assistant could see. I caught it by critiquing my own work honestly when iOS 27 shipped. In version 2, every answer shows where it ran (On iPhone, Private Cloud, or an outside assistant) and nothing acts without your yes. Now I design for trust from day one.
- Real words over grey bars: my Scowtt wireframes used grey bars where words would go, which made reviews abstract. I changed how I work: real content early. The rebuilt Scowtt console uses real copy and adds the one thing my first version lacked, a place to see why each lead ranks.
- Accessibility: in my own audit of my Scowtt palette, white on the taupe button measured 3.3 to 1, under the 4.5 to 1 guideline. A black label keeps the color and fixes it. I check every color pairing now, and my newer work targets WCAG 2.2 AA (Halo ships with it).
- Honest testing: in Halo's tests, version 1 was always shown first, so novelty could have shaped the results. I flagged it in my write-up instead of hiding it, and next time I randomize the order.
- Turning a constraint into a method: I had no budget to travel to Lima for Arriba Perú, so I designed the research itself: six weeks over WhatsApp video with 15 domestic workers and voice-note diaries. Next time I'd also partner with an NGO from day one.
- Owning my work out loud: I used to default to modest framing about what was mine. I've learned to say it plainly, I designed it, I led it, and to be just as clear about what was the team's.
